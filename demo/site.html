mint · the supply side of the skill market

An agent that does a task once with a model, and then never again.

The first run solves the task the ordinary way: a model picks every step. mint writes down not the actions but the shape of them, and that compiles into a small program. Every later run executes that program against data it has never seen, calls no model at all, and checks whether each step did what it promised.

Cost stops being linear in the number of runs. The thousandth run is free, and a refusal costs nothing at all.

Why this is not the twelfth browser agent

Three properties, and each is a thing the alternatives cannot do.

Every piece of this has existed separately. What did not exist is the combination, and the combination is where the economics change.

1 · the selector is a shape

It survives the data changing

Not a path, not a label, not coordinates: the role of the element, the shape of where it points, and its place among the elements that share that shape. That is why a skill learned on one project executes on its siblings — and why a macro, which records a path, breaks.

2 · every step has an obligation

It knows when it failed, and repairs itself

Each step remembers the page it must produce. From that: a mis-click is told apart from a reversal, a step that lands wrong is re-observed and re-taught into the skill, and every step reports which tier resolved it — shape, words, or the model. A run that cannot say where each decision came from cannot be audited.

3 · the model leaves the loop

The answer is absorbed, not discarded

When the model is asked, its answer is compiled into the skill. So cost decays with repetition instead of growing — the opposite of every other agent product, whose cost is linear in the number of runs and whose behaviour never improves on its own.

And what it is not

Not a macro: it survives changed data. Not a cache: it resolves against live state instead of replaying yesterday's answer, and it knows when it cannot. Not a wrapper around a model: the model is a fallback inside the loop, and the loop closes without it.

The model is a part, not the product

Whatever model sits in the loop, it is asked once and then leaves.

The runtime reaches the model through an adapter and speaks two request shapes: the OpenAI one, which covers gateways and most hosted models, and Anthropic's native API, which differs in the address, the auth header and where the answer lives. Both are exercised against a stand-in endpoint that records what it received — the OpenAI shape arriving with a bearer token and a JSON mode flag, the Anthropic shape arriving with x-api-key and anthropic-version and no JSON mode — and both answers are parsed. It has not been run against a live Anthropic key, because we do not hold one; the stand-in is the closest an honest claim can get today.

What it takes

Two shapes cover the field

Point it at an endpoint, name a model, give it a key. Everything specific to a provider lives in one place, and the run is checked end to end after the swap rather than trusted.

What it means for a model vendor

Tokens stop being a recurring cost

Every other agent asks the model again on every run, forever, and the bill grows with usage. Here the model is asked once per task and then leaves the loop: the same credits buy a permanent capability instead of being consumed again tomorrow. For whoever supplies the model, that is capital rather than operating expense.

Why it matters here

The compiled path needs no vendor at all

A rate limit, a credit limit, a revoked key or a dead endpoint stops the first run only. That is not resilience bolted on afterwards; it falls out of the model not being in the loop — and it is why the same runtime is the only agent that can run on a machine with no network.

Where it can be pointed

The compiler does not know what a browser is.

It learns three things and nothing else: recognise the target by its shape, act on it, check the result it was meant to produce. Everything else is an adapter — what counts as state, and what counts as an action. The browser came first because it is the hardest surface: the page redraws under you, the contents change, nothing is versioned. Every other surface below is easier than the one already working.

Into the ARLI family

The trade has three lines. The step where the work happens is missing.

ARLI is already three things. Commons is the pod where a team and the agents each of them runs work together. Match finds who overlaps with you without publishing your list. Connect measures whether an industrial catalogue can be read by a buying agent, and issues a signed receipt for every step of a trade. Read as one trade: commons is where the work lives, match is who you meet, connect is what can be read — and then nobody does the work. Placing an order in a supplier's portal, filing a declaration, registering an entity: no line covers it.

the gap

Connect receipts the reading. Nothing receipts the doing.

Connect already issues a signed receipt for every step of a trade, for a catalogue being read. The same shape of receipt can be issued for a procedure being executed: the page a step had to reach, the page it reached, and which tier resolved it. Together those two cover the whole trade — your catalogue answered me correctly and the order was really placed.

the line we add

Mint — one die, unlimited coins at a known cost, a stamp on each

A mint strikes the same coin from one die as often as you like, and every coin carries its stamp. A demonstrated task is the die; every later run on new data is a coin; the tier breakdown is the stamp. That is the layer that is missing, and the reason it can be missing everywhere else: striking costs almost nothing per coin, so it is the only layer in this chain that does not get more expensive the more it is used. A unit whose production cost is known and whose result is provable is also exactly what a market needs in order to price one.

The line is called mint, and so is the runtime that does the striking: one name for the one thing, and every number on this page came out of it.

economics

The same price, a different business

A knowledge skill's author pays a model on every answer out of their 70% share. A cast procedure's author pays nothing per call: the work was paid for once, at learning time. At the same price in the same catalogue, one keeps what is left after inference and the other keeps nearly all of it. A marketplace feels that on the first invoice.

Measured: the call above answered with 0 model calls and returned a receipt.

refusal

A refusal here costs nothing, because nothing is spent

Measured on this marketplace: any answer is charged, including a negative one — a "no match" cost $0.50, because a wrong question still burns the author's inference. Only HTTP refusals are free. So a caller must aim before spending. A casting that cannot be aimed is refused before any work starts, and that is not a policy promise: there is no model in the path, so there is nothing to spend.

Built and measured: a call with no target returned charged_usd: 0 and the note that no model call was made.

This category already exists. Here is who is in it.

Saying otherwise would be the easiest lie to catch, so it is said first: record once with a model, replay deterministically without one, is not a new idea. It is a documented pattern, a research sub-field, and in some places a shipping product.

closest

terx and maponce

terx records a semantic workflow after an agent succeeds and replays it over CDP with no model call. It requires declared preconditions and postconditions, and it refuses instead of guessing. maponce maps a page once into a YAML object repository and replays with deterministic Playwright at zero tokens, healing through stored selectors, then role and accessible name, then fuzzy metadata, and only then the model on a shortlist. It also replays a fresh recording with healing off to check it reproduces.

Both MIT, both created in 2026 by a single author, both at zero stars. Neither pairs the compiled procedure with a market.

also shipping

A crowded neighbourhood

browser-agent saves a plan with xpath and cssPath and replays with no model calls. Hyperbrowser caches actions and can emit a script from the cache. imprint compiles a recorded session into an MCP server and says plainly that the recording is the executable. Separately, self-healing selectors are an industry with three named generations, and the newest generation addresses elements by natural-language description.

the research line

Studied, benchmarked, and shipped to production

Voyager writes executable skills into a retrievable library. Agent Workflow Memory induces reusable workflows from trajectories and is benchmarked on WebArena and Mind2Web; MemP, LEGOMem and ExpVoyager extend it. LILO and DreamCoder compress solved programs into reusable abstractions. Procedural memory is now a production feature in at least one major agent product.

what is actually ours

Not the mechanism. The target, the measurement, and the market.

1. Tuned for transfer between instances, not drift within one. Healing tools repair the same application after it changes. This addresses an element by its shape and was measured on twenty instances it had never seen, of a class it learned from one.

2. The measurement is published, failures included. We did not find a class-scale transfer measurement with named failures in any of the above; most state zero-token replay without one. Ours: 56 of 60 arrived, four misses named, and one skill that collapses to 1 of 20 with no model available.

3. The market. Nobody in that list connects a struck procedure to a catalogue that sells it, with a receipt shaped like the one Connect already issues. That part is not a mechanism. It is a decision, and it is still open.

What a run can prove

A receipt for the work, not a promise that it was done.

Anyone can say a task ran. The question worth answering is what a third party can check afterwards without trusting the runtime, the person running it, or us. Every step now crosses a gate that writes down what it is about to do, and the record is signed and chained, so it can be checked offline.

before the effect

The decision is written first.

A step is judged by what it would do, never by what it says: a link goes somewhere, typing fills a field, a button commits. Reads pass. Anything that commits is held until an approval bound to that exact action arrives, and it is released once. What cannot be classified is refused, because there is nothing to describe to a person and therefore nothing to approve.

A refusal is recorded before anything could have happened. That is the difference between saying a step was stopped and showing a record that was signed ahead of it.

after the effect

Signed, chained, and checkable by someone else.

Every decision and every outcome is a signed receipt: Ed25519, hash-chained so each one commits to the one before it. An action that was performed has two — the decision before it and the outcome after. An action that was refused has the decision and nothing else, and that absence is the evidence. The verifier runs offline and asks nothing of us.

Measured on real runs: 18 runs signed, 48 receipts, every signature and every link holding. Three ways of lying to it — a line edited, a receipt deleted, two reordered — caught at the receipt where the chain first disagrees.

the rules, in the open

What was decided is not taken on trust either.

The rules that decide are a document, not branches in the code: which observations make a step a read, which make it a write, what a write with no approval gets, what an unclassifiable step gets. Its hash goes into every receipt, and the document is published beside the log. A verifier applies the published rules to the observations recorded in each receipt and checks that the same verdict comes back out.

That is what catches a runtime doing what its own rules forbid. Measured: a held write rewritten to “allow” and then re-signed with the right key — valid signatures, an intact chain — is caught, because the rules say hold. Being stricter than the published rules is allowed. Being laxer is not.

who accepted the rules

A counter-signature, and what it does not fix.

A second key, not the runtime's, signs the policy hash for this runtime and this scope, with an optional expiry. The verifier refuses an endorsement signed by the runtime's own key, one covering a different policy, one naming a different runtime, and one that has run out — because a self-endorsement reported as a counter-signature is worse than none at all, and a reader would stop looking for the missing half.

What it does not fix: the counterparty. The strength of the claim is exactly the strength of whoever holds the second key, and no amount of code changes that. Both keys in this demonstration are held on this machine, so it shows the mechanism and says nothing about trust. The timestamps are still the signer's clock.

What a receipt claims, in the words the receipt itself carries: “A receipt proves that this record was issued by the holder of the stated key and has not been altered since. It does not prove the action was correct, that its inputs were true, that a person approved it, or that the world changed the way the record describes.” A receipt that promises more than it can support is worse than no receipt, because a reader cannot tell where the support ends.

Watch it decide: four elements and four kinds of decision — a link, a field, a button, and one thing that cannot be classified at all.

Check it yourself. The signed log from that demonstration is here, the rules it was decided under are here, and the counterparty's signature on those rules is here. Checking them needs nothing from us: mint receipts --in receipts.json --policy policy.json --endorsement endorsement.json verifies every signature and every link, replays the published rules against every recorded decision, checks that somebody other than the runtime accepted those rules, and reports the receipt where anything first disagrees.

Nine ways of lying to it all fail that command. Five on the log: a line edited, a receipt deleted, two reordered, a verdict rewritten with the whole chain re-signed by the correct key, and the rules themselves swapped out afterwards. Four on the endorsement: signed by the runtime's own key, covering a different policy, naming a different runtime, and expired. Each is rejected for its own reason.

Take it, or take it apart

Nothing here needs our permission, and most of it does not need our code.

The runtime, the gate, the receipts and the rules engine are one repository under Apache-2.0: github.com/dmitrypehovski/mint. There are three ways in, and they cost different amounts of trust in us — the third costs none.

one · run it

Build it and check the log we publish.

cargo build --release, then three files and one command verify everything on this site: every signature, every link in the chain, every verdict re-derived from the published rules, and the counter-signature. Nothing is fetched from us at check time, and nothing about the check depends on the program that produced the log.

The demonstrations at /demos print the exact command behind each one, including the four that run the real verifier while you watch.

two · embed it

Put the gate in your own agent.

The four pieces are separable on purpose, and the library exposes each. Embedding the gate is not a matter of remembering to call it: it is a required argument of the only function that can touch a page, so leaving it out is a compile error rather than a line somebody forgot. When this was written, that arrangement found five places in this codebase where steps were going around the gate that we believed were already closed.

The rules engine also stands alone: mint rules --policy p.json --observations o.json applies a rules document to observations and prints the verdicts, with no browser, no model and no agent involved. That is the command the first demonstration calls when you flip its switch.

three · implement the format

Make your own, and let the two check each other.

The receipt format and the rules document are specified field by field in docs/: the canonical bytes that are signed, what each field asserts, how a link is computed and what it references, and which checks a verifier has to make in which order. Enough for another language to produce receipts this verifier accepts, and to verify ours.

This is the one that matters. A format with a single implementation is a product, not a standard — and the whole claim here is that a stranger with none of our code can check our work. That claim is only true if the format is the interface.

Where it drops into the family

Three lines exist, and the step where the work actually happens was missing from all of them. This is what the missing step would attach to, and what each attachment would need.

commons

A skill sold there returns a receipt with its result.

Not a claim that the skill ran — a signed record of what it did, which the buyer keeps and can check without asking us. And because the rules are a document rather than branches in code, the pod can agree on one published policy and check every member's log against it, which turns "we are careful about what our agents do" into something a member can demonstrate.

What it needs: a return channel from a call to a receipt. That is a contract change, not a research problem. Publishing into the catalogue today additionally needs an author dashboard token, which we do not hold.

connect

The two halves of one trade, in the same shape.

Connect issues a signed receipt for every step of a trade being read. Mint issues one for a procedure being executed. Together they cover the whole thing: your catalogue answered me correctly and the order was really placed, each checkable by the party that did not produce it.

What it needs: agreement that both receipts describe one trade — a shared identifier, and a decision about which of them a dispute is settled by.

match

Evidence instead of a self-report.

"Who has actually done this" is currently answered by whoever is willing to say so. A receipt makes it answerable: this runtime did this, under these rules, at this time, and here is the record. Overlap stops being a claim two parties make about each other.

What it needs: deciding what may be shown from a receipt without showing the work — the observations and the verdict are enough to prove a procedure ran and often not enough to reveal what was in it.

and the one thing code cannot supply

Somebody holding the other key.

Every key on this site was generated on this machine, so every demonstration here proves the mechanism and says nothing about trust. A real endorsement takes a person or an organisation who was asked and agreed, holding a key that is not the runtime's. In the family that is a decision about who is willing to be the counterparty, not a build item.

Until somebody is, the honest reading of these pages is: the machinery works, and nobody outside has yet vouched for it.

What is not built

None of the three integrations above exists yet. There is no live link between this runtime and commons, connect or match. What exists is the runtime, the format, the published artifacts and a demonstration that the mechanism holds under attempts to break it. The specifications are in the repository's docs/ so that the integration can be written by whoever wants it, including somebody who is not us.

What the speed unlocks

It does not make the same things faster. It changes what you can attempt.

Every consequence below follows from one number: a decision costing — instead of —. Each is something nobody attempts today, because at the old price it is absurd.

1 · verify instead of sample

You check everything, because checking is free

Audit practice samples because each check costs money. At — you verify all five hundred pages after a migration instead of fifty, all tenants instead of the loudest one. Sampling exists because verification is expensive; remove the expense and the idea of a sample disappears with it.

2 · search your own strategies

The runtime can try a hundred paths and keep the best

If trying costs nothing, compiling one variant is a waste. Compile several, benchmark them against live pages, keep the fastest and steadiest — the runtime searching its own skill space. Already visible without anyone searching: the compiled skills reach the goal in one step where the model needed two.

3 · agents inside a request

An agent you can call in the request path

Nobody puts an agent inside a web request today: — per step makes it impossible. At — the compiled path sits in the request path of ordinary software — checking a form, resolving a record, validating a submission — the way any other function does.

4 · continuous, not periodic

Drift detection every five minutes instead of every quarter

Interface-drift monitoring is a subscription nobody buys, because each crawl costs money. At zero marginal cost a canary runs continuously, and reports not "the page changed" but "a working process broke, and here is the step" — because a skill that fails to resolve one step is exactly that signal.

5 · cost per shape

Pricing flips from per-execution to per-shape

If the thousandth run is free, the cost of a task is paid once and amortised over everything after it. In a market where agents are paid per task, the agent that has done the work before undercuts everyone without giving up margin. That is an economic property, and it is the one that decides who wins.

6 · no network in the decision

An agent that works with the network off

The compiled path makes no model call, so a rate limit, a credit limit, a revoked key or a dead provider cannot touch it. That is not degraded mode, it is the full function — the only way an agent runs in defence, medicine, industry or the field, and a configuration nobody builds because everyone assumed a model on every step.

Evidence for the sceptic

Every number on this page came out of a run.

Collected — on the machine that serves this page. Raw files: evaluation.json, measurements.json.

Observation is not the bottleneck. The model is.

The accessibility tree is what a conventional agent sends the model: an order of magnitude more bytes for strictly less information, because it does not carry the address by which a step is recognised. Our state payload is smaller and more useful — which is the whole reason the selector can be a shape at all.

The decision, per step

The skills themselves

A skill is under a kilobyte and plain JSON. It fits in a code review, which is what makes it signable — see the application "approved before it runs" above.

What existed before this

Already existed

  • Learn-a-macro-from-a-demonstration — RPA, twenty years old. Fails when the data changes.
  • Recorded selectors — Selenium and Playwright codegen. Fail faster: tied to the markup.
  • A model at every step — browser-use, computer-use, Operator. Works on new data, pays forever.
  • Semantic caching — a cache of text, not of actions. Does not help.

Not existed before

  • A shape that transfers between instances — one skill, many targets, no adjustment.
  • Steps that carry obligations — self-diagnosis, self-repair, a named tier per decision.
  • A model whose answers are compiled away — cost that falls with use instead of rising.

How it works, in three layers

Observe

Read the DOM, not the tree

The tree does not carry href, and without it a step cannot be recognised on a page whose contents changed. Elements are addressed by handle: no coordinates are computed at all, so there is nothing to mis-aim when the layout shifts.

Compile

The trajectory becomes a program

Role, the shape of where it points, its place among its peers, and the page it must produce. Plain readable JSON.

Execute

Recognise, act, verify, heal

The tier is named for every step. A miss is a question, not a failure — and the correction is written back so the next run does not have to ask.

Not proven

What we have not shown, stated before anyone asks.

A claim that arrives without its limits is a claim nobody should trust. These are ours, and each is a measurement we have not made yet rather than a doubt we are hiding.

One surface is built, seven are argued

The browser adapter is measured; the other seven are the same compiler with a new adapter, which is a claim about architecture, not a result. Until one of them is built, treat the other surfaces as engineering work rather than a capability.

Whole-task speedup is far smaller than per-decision

The chain runs about three times faster end to end, not thousands: page loads dominate, and nobody can speed those up. We improved the thinking, not the network.

One skill is not verified

The "latest commit" skill lands on a commit page, which the structural verdict accepts, but the semantic judge says it opened a commit rather than the latest one. It is marked unverified inside the runtime, and that is why that task is the one that still costs a question to the model.

Decisions scale with page size

On light pages a decision cost 30 µs; on these heavier pages the median is — and the worst is 283 µs. Still three orders of magnitude under the model, but it is not a constant.

Transfer works inside one family

All six targets share a shape of site. A different family means new learning: cheap, but not free. Proving the class needs three real forms of one kind — the next experiment.

Judgement tasks are out of scope

Reading an unfamiliar document and deciding what to do is not a shape. Nothing to compile, and we are not pretending otherwise.
