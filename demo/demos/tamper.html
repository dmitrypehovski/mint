demonstration 3 · live

Five ways to lie to the log. All five get caught.

The log is signed with Ed25519 and each receipt is chained to the one before it. But signatures only stop people who do not have the key. The interesting case is the fifth one, and pressing a button runs the real verifier on a real tampered copy — this is not a video of a verifier.

Why the fifth is the hard one

  
If you hold the key, you can rewrite history and re-sign it, and every cryptographic
  check passes. Nothing in the chain can tell the difference, because there is no difference — the
  bytes are authentic. What catches it is that the receipt recorded the observations, not just
  the verdict. Re-apply the published rules to those observations and you get hold where the
  log says allow. Authentic, and wrong.

  
Why the third way of lying is invisible without a chain

  
Deleting or reordering receipts does not make any signature invalid. Each receipt is
  still perfectly signed. What breaks is the link: a receipt points at the hash of the one before it,
  and that pointer no longer describes the file it is in. Without the chain, an audit could only count
  receipts — never place them in an order.




the command: mint receipts --in receipts.json --policy policy.json (add --tamper DIR and the binary writes all five forged copies itself, then
checks them)


what this does not show
It does not show that the verifier is infallible. It shows which specific ways of lying it catches, and the list is finite and stated. A lie not on this list is not caught by this check — and the honest answer to “what about a lie you did not think of” is that the log records observations precisely so somebody else can catch the ones we missed.
